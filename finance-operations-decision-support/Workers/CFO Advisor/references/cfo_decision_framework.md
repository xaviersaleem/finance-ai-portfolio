# CFO Decision Framework

How the CFO Advisor reviews the prior work, challenges it, and reaches a defensible board recommendation on the one question: **should Crave Kitchen Group open 10 new locations next year?**

## 1. The CFO's value-add

The analysts gave you numbers; your job is judgment. You add value by (a) pressure-testing what was handed up, (b) connecting the finance to strategy and to the board's own decision criteria, and (c) committing to a clear, accountable recommendation. Directors are not paying you to summarize — they are paying you to decide and to defend the decision.

## 2. Reviewing the evidence

- **From worker 1 (Store_Analysis_Report):** Is the current base healthy enough to expand from? Look at blended EBITDA margin, prime-cost control, consistency across stores, and which regions have a proven, repeatable model. Expanding off a weak or inconsistent base is the classic value-destroying mistake.
- **From worker 2 (Expansion_Business_Case):** For the **10-store scenario**, note NPV at the hurdle rate, ROI vs the minimum, IRR vs the hurdle, payback vs the maximum, profitability index, pass/fail, the key sensitivities, and the leading risks. Also note which scenario worker 2 recommended and why.
- **From the Board Investment Framework:** the criteria and thresholds directors actually apply, and how they expect investments framed.

## 3. Challenging the assumptions

Accept nothing at face value — this is the heart of the role.
- **Revenue realism:** Does the assumed revenue per new store square with worker 1's *actual* per-store revenue, especially in the regions expansion targets? Forecasts that exceed the current best stores deserve skepticism.
- **Ramp:** New stores take time to mature. If the case assumes fast ramp, payback flatters reality. Ask what payback looks like under a slower ramp.
- **Margins:** Will new stores hit mature margins, or carry opening drag? Are inflation/labor assumptions conservative enough given the market file?
- **Capital:** Is total capex within the board's appetite and the company's financing capacity? A great return the company can't fund is not actionable.
- **Scenario choice:** The board asked about 10 stores. If worker 2 favored a different scenario (5 or 15), confront it: is 10 a reasonable middle, an overreach, or under-ambitious given the evidence? Say so.

Record where you agree, where you discount the case, and the reasoning. Visible, fair challenge is what makes your eventual recommendation credible.

## 4. Strategic fit

Money is necessary but not sufficient. Using the Executive Briefing: does opening 10 locations advance the stated strategy and leadership priorities? Does it build on genuine strengths (strong regions, brand, unit economics) or stretch into unproven territory? Strategic fit can justify a marginal financial case — or veto a strong one that pulls the company off course.

## 5. Risk versus reward

Weigh the upside (NPV, value creation, market capture, momentum) against the downside (execution risk of opening many units at once, demand/ramp risk, cost inflation, concentration, financing/liquidity risk). Tie risks to worker 2's sensitivities — the variable the case is most sensitive to is the risk to manage. Frame reward as risk-adjusted, not best-case. A board wants to know what happens if it goes wrong, and whether the company can absorb that.

## 6. Capital allocation

Judge the decision as a use of scarce capital. Does 10 stores clear the hurdle with room to spare, or only just? Is capital efficiency (NPV per dollar, payback) competitive with the company's other uses of cash? Could phasing (e.g., open in tranches with go/no-go gates) capture most of the value at lower risk? Capital-allocation discipline is the CFO's signature contribution.

## 7. Reaching the recommendation

Commit to one of three clear stances, and say which:
- **Proceed** — open 10 next year. The base is strong, the 10-store case clears the framework with acceptable risk, and it fits strategy.
- **Proceed with conditions** — open 10, but gated (phased rollout, financing secured first, target the proven regions, hold KPIs). Often the most credible CFO answer; still a yes.
- **Do not proceed (this year)** — the base, the returns, the risk, or the capital position don't support 10 now. Offer the alternative (a smaller scenario, a delay, fix the base first).

Whatever you choose, it must be unambiguous and consistent everywhere it appears in the deck. "It depends" is not a decision. Decide, then defend.

## 8. Writing for the board

- **Lead with the answer.** State the recommendation in the Executive Summary and restate it on the Final Decision slide. Directors should never search for your call.
- **Be concise and quantified.** Short sentences, key numbers, no analyst jargon. One idea per line.
- **Be honest about risk.** Surfacing the real risks and your mitigation builds trust; burying them destroys it.
- **Cite the evidence.** Supporting Rationale should explicitly reference worker 1 and worker 2's findings so the call is defensible under questioning.
- **Sound like a CFO.** Calm, confident, accountable. You are recommending, and you stand behind it.
